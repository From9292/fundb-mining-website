1. Blockchain basics

A blockchain is a shared record of transactions maintained by a network of computers. Instead of relying on one organisation to keep the only copy, participating computers follow a common set of rules to agree on the order and validity of records. Different blockchains use different technical designs, fees, security assumptions and governance arrangements.

What a blockchain does—and does not do

  • Records activity: a public blockchain can let people inspect transactions and contract activity using a block explorer.
  • Uses network rules: validators or miners help the network agree on valid records, depending on its consensus design.
  • Does not guarantee value: recording a token on a blockchain does not create demand, a market price, a buyer or a guaranteed return.
  • Does not reverse every mistake: sending assets to a wrong or incompatible address can result in permanent loss.

Before using a blockchain application, identify the exact network, the asset's contract address where applicable, the transaction fees and the official source for checking transactions. Similar token names can exist on different networks and may have no relationship to one another.

2. Wallets, private keys and recovery phrases

A crypto wallet is a tool for managing the keys that authorize blockchain transactions. The assets themselves are recorded on the network; the wallet uses cryptographic keys to prove that a transaction is authorized. A wallet address can usually be shared to receive a supported asset, but a private key or recovery phrase must remain secret.

Basic safety checklist

  1. Install wallet software only from the wallet provider's verified official website or official app-store listing.
  2. Write a recovery phrase offline and store it somewhere private and secure. Do not photograph it, post it in a chat or save it in an unprotected cloud note.
  3. Never type a recovery phrase into a website, support form, social-media message or an app sent by a stranger.
  4. Check the network and the first and last characters of a destination address before sending. For a new recipient, consider a small test transfer if fees and circumstances make that sensible.
  5. Keep your phone, operating system and wallet application updated, and use a strong device lock.

Support staff do not need your private key or recovery phrase to investigate a normal account question. Anyone who asks for them should be treated as suspicious. A wallet provider may be able to help with its own software, but it cannot simply reverse a confirmed blockchain transfer.

Custodial and self-custody services

With a custodial service, a provider controls the keys and is responsible for safeguarding access under its terms and applicable rules. With self-custody, the user controls the keys and is responsible for backups and transaction approvals. Neither model removes risk. Read the provider's terms and understand who can authorize transactions, freeze access or help recover an account.

3. Recognising online and crypto scams

Scams often use urgency, impersonation and promises that sound unusually certain. A polished logo, a large social-media following, a referral link or a screenshot of a balance is not independent proof that a service is legitimate or that money can be withdrawn.

Warning signs

  • A guaranteed daily income, fixed return or risk-free profit from cryptocurrency.
  • A demand to pay an activation, tax, verification or unlock fee before a withdrawal can be released.
  • A request for your password, one-time code, private key or recovery phrase.
  • Pressure to recruit friends as the main source of income, or rewards that depend on buying a package.
  • Unverified customer-support accounts, copied websites, shortened links or an address that changes without a clear explanation.

How to verify a claim

  1. Open the service's official website by typing its known address yourself rather than following an unexpected message.
  2. Find the legal operator, contact details, terms, privacy notice and a clear explanation of how the service earns revenue.
  3. For a token, look for the official contract address and independently inspect it using the correct network's block explorer.
  4. Look for a real transaction hash on the relevant blockchain. A screenshot or an in-app number alone is not proof of an on-chain payment.
  5. Do not send money you cannot afford to lose. Pause and ask a trusted person to review an unfamiliar offer.

If you believe you have been scammed, stop sending funds, preserve messages and transaction details, contact the relevant wallet or exchange through its official support channel, and report the incident to the appropriate authorities in your country.

4. Understanding cryptocurrency mining claims

In proof-of-work networks, mining is a competitive computing process that helps order transactions and secure the network. Mining requires hardware, electricity, network access and operating costs. A phone application that displays a growing number is not necessarily performing proof-of-work mining or earning cryptocurrency on a public blockchain.

Other applications use the word “mining” to describe points, simulated balances, cloud contracts, referrals or future reward programmes. These are different activities and should be described accurately. Before participating, ask what work is being performed, who operates the infrastructure, how rewards are funded, what fees apply and whether withdrawals have actually been enabled.

Do not assume that a displayed balance is cash, a token, a debt owed to you or an amount that can be withdrawn. Check the published rules and the actual transaction evidence. A project should clearly disclose when a feature is a preview, test or future proposal.

5. Networks, addresses and transaction fees

Blockchain networks are separate systems. An address format may look similar across networks, but that does not mean every asset can be sent safely between them. A token may also exist as separate contracts on multiple networks. Always confirm the network selected by both the sending and receiving services.

Before confirming a transfer

  • Confirm the asset name and contract address from a trusted official source.
  • Confirm the network on both sides of the transfer.
  • Check the destination address carefully and understand whether a memo or tag is required.
  • Review the network fee and the amount the recipient is expected to receive.
  • Understand that a pending transaction can take time and that a confirmed transaction is generally irreversible.

Never rely on a search advertisement or a random message to obtain a deposit address. Use the receiving service's authenticated deposit screen and follow its network instructions. If a service says deposits or withdrawals are not yet available, do not send assets to an address claiming to belong to it.

6. A practical checklist for evaluating a digital project

Take time to evaluate a project before creating an account, sharing personal information or sending assets. A useful review looks beyond branding and promotional claims.

  1. Identity: Can you identify the people or organisation operating the service and find reliable contact information?
  2. Functionality: Can you test the advertised features, or are they only screenshots, mock-ups or roadmap items?
  3. Economics: Is the source of rewards explained? Are fees, eligibility, limits and risks disclosed?
  4. Security: Does the service explain authentication, data handling, account recovery and how it protects keys?
  5. Independent evidence: Are claims supported by verifiable records rather than testimonials or referral promotions?
  6. Policies: Are the privacy notice, terms, advertising disclosures and complaint channels easy to find?
  7. Exit: Can you understand how to close an account, withdraw eligible funds or request help—and what happens if a service stops operating?

Be cautious when important answers are missing. Do not treat a roadmap, audit claim, partnership logo or future launch date as proof that a feature is already available.
